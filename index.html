About

I grew up taking things apart to understand how they worked, and that habit never really left me. I studied industrial design at Srishti Manipal Institute of Art, Design and Technology in Bangalore, and the eight years since have looked less like a straight career and more like a string of very curious detours.

I've designed furniture shown at the India Art Fair, built a haptic controller for a VR cricket game, turned a room of vintage telephones into an interactive museum installation, and helped design two very different kinds of care — a mental wellbeing platform at Microsoft Research, and a healthcare app connecting patients with providers.

The chapter that shaped me most, though, is Karya. For two years I led Karya Learn, a program helping rural data annotators in India build new skills and financial confidence at the same time — and watching that actually work, then writing about why it worked, taught me more about design than any studio brief has.

What ties all of this together is a stubborn belief that the best answers come from actually being where people are, not guessing from a distance — whether that's a village in rural India or a client's living room.

Lately, that same curiosity has pointed me somewhere new. After years of designing for screens, I want to know materials the way I once wanted to know how things worked — by getting my hands into them. Natural building, specifically, feels less like a departure and more like the same question I've always been asking, just asked in earth and wood instead of pixels.
